THE MATH / GROSS VS NET / 9 MIN

Break-even is more than your buy price

A trade can show a gross gain and still make a smaller net gain, or a loss, after charges. Follow a full example and test how costs change the sale price needed to get back to zero.

THE QUICK TAKE

Gross P/L looks only at buy and sell prices. Net P/L also subtracts the charges paid to enter and exit. Break-even is where that net result reaches zero.

Gross profit is not take-home profit

Imagine a fictional trade: 100 shares of Company ABC bought for ₹100 each. Assume ₹15 of entry costs and ₹25 of exit costs, held flat here only to keep the arithmetic easy.

Calculate a simple break-even price

For fixed total charges, add the assumed entry and exit costs to your purchase value, then divide by the number of shares. In this example: (₹10,000 + ₹40) ÷ 100 = ₹100.40 per share.

Estimated break-even = (purchase value + total entry and exit costs) ÷ shares

Break-even and target profit are different questions

Break-even asks for the sale price where the estimated net result reaches zero. A target-profit calculation starts from the same ledger but adds the amount you want to keep after costs. With 100 shares bought at ₹100, ₹40 of assumed total charges, and a ₹1,000 net-profit target, the sale needs to raise ₹11,040 in total: ₹10,000 to recover the purchase, ₹40 for costs, and ₹1,000 for the target.

Why small costs can matter differently to different trades

Costs should be read relative to the trade, not just as a rupee total. A fixed ₹40 charge is 0.4% of a ₹10,000 purchase value before considering how charges scale. The same charge is 0.04% of ₹1,00,000. For a low-price move or a short holding period, the cost hurdle can consume a larger share of the gross return. For a much larger move, it may be a smaller share, but it does not disappear.

Turnover-based charges also mean that doubling quantity can increase both potential profit and transaction costs. A larger position is not automatically more efficient: compare the estimated cost per share and the amount of capital exposed before drawing that conclusion.

Do not let break-even become an anchor

Your original purchase price is part of your record, but the market does not know what you paid. Waiting for the exact break-even price can become an emotional rule rather than a fresh assessment. The relevant decision is not only “Can the price return to my cost?” but also “Given what I know now, does holding this position still fit my plan better than the alternatives?”

This does not mean ignoring cost basis. It means using it for accounting and performance measurement without treating it as evidence that a future recovery is due. A break-even calculator cannot assess a company’s value, predict a price path, compare opportunity costs, or tell you what to do with a holding.

What costs should you check?

  • Brokerage: your broker’s fee for the orders, subject to the plan and product.
  • Exchange and regulatory charges: charges that can depend on the venue, turnover, and current rules.
  • Taxes and duties: applicable taxes can differ by product and transaction type.
  • Other frictions: bid-ask spread, slippage, and price movement while an order is being filled.

Some costs are percentage-based, capped, or rounded rather than fixed. If a charge changes with the sale value, the exact break-even may need an iterative calculation. Check current broker rates and applicable rules; do not copy the flat ₹40 example as a real estimate.

A practical review before relying on the estimate

  • Confirm that the calculation includes both entry and exit, not only the buy-side fee.
  • Use the correct product, exchange, brokerage plan, and current charge schedule.
  • Check whether any input is a flat amount, percentage, cap, or rounded charge.
  • Keep taxes, spreads, slippage, and other excluded items visible rather than assuming they are zero.
  • Compare the estimated net outcome with the risk and alternatives; do not treat break-even as a forecast.

The estimate is most useful as a transparent accounting scenario: it makes assumptions visible and lets you test how sensitive the answer is to quantity, fees, and target. For an actual trade, reconcile against your broker’s contract note and current rate schedule.

Company ABC, prices, and charges in this guide are fictional. Break-even is an accounting estimate, not a price forecast or a promise of profit.
